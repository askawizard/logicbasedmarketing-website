Case studies / Product master data

One product master across six systems, with SKUs rebuilt for ERP

SKUs didn't match across QuickBooks and the WooCommerce stores, and no system held one complete record of a product's dimensions, weight, UPC and cost. I rebuilt 1,021 SKUs under one ERP-ready convention, then joined 10,642 SKUs across six systems and measured the real data gap before an ERP load.

A B2B industrial equipment company preparing an ERP migration and running a warehouse, with product data split across QuickBooks, two WooCommerce storefronts, Salesforce, ShipStation, an inventory tracker and a spreadsheet. I owned the data work; ERP field provisioning sat with the operations lead. Designed and built the SKU rebuild toolchain and the product master pipeline.

The problem

SKUs were the link between every system, and they didn't match: special characters and spaces, capacity written different ways, and 25 collision groups where one SKU string held different data in different places. The incoming ERP requires every SKU to be 31 characters or fewer. Past SKUs, no system held a complete product record. Dimensions, weight and UPC lived in exactly one spreadsheet, and ShipStation, long assumed to be a source, returned null weight, dimensions and cost for all 710 of its products. Against the core catalog, only 6% of products had a complete record.

What I built

I built a Python toolchain that pulled every product from QuickBooks and both WooCommerce stores, built an old-to-new mapping under one convention, applied the renames through the APIs and verified they landed. The convention is alphanumeric and hyphens only, at most 31 characters, with capacity encoded as a numeric suffix in pounds and plus-variants written as -PLUS so a real product difference is never stripped. Collisions were settled by one reproducible rule: the SKU with the highest WooCommerce sales keeps the canonical name.

For the master, I built a re-runnable, read-only pipeline joining QuickBooks, both storefronts, Salesforce, ShipStation, the inventory tracker and the UPC spreadsheet into one workbook. I tiered the catalog so coverage means something, defining 2,422 core SKUs by recent sales, the UPC sheet, live listings or the physical count, and measured only physical products. Then I verified the real source for each field instead of assuming it.

Cost came from QuickBooks purchase history rather than a sparse cost field: 27,673 dated line items across 1,999 items. Along the way I found that a QuickBooks item query with an explicit column list silently drops the SKU field, which makes QuickBooks look like it has no SKUs. The rule now is to select all columns and filter in code.

Decisions that mattered

  • Collisions went to the SKU with the highest WooCommerce sales, so every outcome was reproducible instead of a case-by-case judgment call.
  • Plus-variants became -PLUS rather than being stripped, because the plus sign marks a real product difference.
  • I measured coverage against core physical products only, since counting every dead listing and fee line would have produced a meaningless number.
  • I reported 6% complete as the finding instead of dressing up partial coverage, because a measured baseline is the only number anyone can act on.

How the result was checked

A post-deploy script confirmed the renames landed in both QuickBooks and WooCommerce, and a mapping workbook records every old-to-new change. Each field's source was verified live against its system rather than assumed. Before any ERP load, I flagged 11 rows in the supplied spreadsheet whose name, dimensions and UPC belonged to a different model, including a 700 lb freight error.
