Case studies / Order-to-cash integrity

Web orders book themselves, and every stalled order surfaces with a fix

Web orders were re-keyed into QuickBooks by hand, and an order could stall between payment, Salesforce and the warehouse without anyone being alerted. I built a reversible order sync and a set of gap scanners under one dashboard that surfaced $354K of in-flight orders at launch and cleared the paid-but-not-closed backlog to 0.

A B2B industrial equipment company selling by web, phone and dealers, with orders and money moving through WooCommerce, Stripe, QuickBooks, Salesforce and ShipStation. I owned the systems layer between them; the warehouse and accounting teams work from the boards I built. Designed and built the order sync, every scanner and the Order Manager dashboard.

The problem

Every web order was re-keyed into QuickBooks by hand, and only about 2% of QuickBooks items carried a SKU, so products could not be matched between systems. Past the books, orders could stall in the gaps: a paid order never sent to shipping, an ACH or wire payment that never closed the Salesforce opportunity, a payment-link order that never reached the warehouse. Measured from production logs, 9 of 299 payment-link orders were never submitted to fulfillment and 4 more went late. Shipping status had no authority either: against orders known to have shipped, ShipStation's status was wrong 18% of the time and WooCommerce's 30%.

What I built

I started with product matching. Batch scripts created the missing QuickBooks items with a SKU on each, checking exact SKU, exact name, normalized name and fuzzy matches before every create, which prevented 1,056 duplicates and introduced 0 new ones. Then I built the order sync around one rule: never corrupt the books. It runs forward-only on a single worker, records every receipt in a rollback ledger so a bad sync can be reversed, pre-creates missing items so a receipt can't silently drop, and scrubs wallet-payment billing data so customers get their real address instead of the payment processor's.

Next I covered the gaps between systems, one scanner per failure mode: paid orders with no tracking, payments that never closed the Salesforce opportunity, payment-link orders that never reached fulfillment, and Stripe charges with no QuickBooks receipt. Each alerts on a new problem, re-notifies once a day until it is fixed and offers the button that resolves it. An Order Manager dashboard puts one link over all of them and holds no QuickBooks or Salesforce credentials; it proxies each action back to the service that owns it.

For shipping, I made the QuickBooks tracking box the single authority, and a number there means a label was generated, nothing more. The scanner reads both fields the team types into, uses ShipStation as a second witness and writes a corroborated match back into QuickBooks without ever overwriting a hand-typed number.

Decisions that mattered

  • Payment-link orders stayed on a manual path by choice, so I built a board that keeps re-nagging instead of automating a path with known variance.
  • The scanners never auto-close a deal or merge records, because which win survives is a credit decision for a person.
  • I refused to match money on amount alone after real web orders charged amounts that differed from their receipts, since an alert channel that cries wolf gets muted.
  • I built the dashboard as an aggregator instead of rewriting the scanners, so each keeps its own edge cases and the new view adds no credentials to protect.

How the result was checked

The receipt scanner's go-live baseline reconciled 172 charges: 147 matched, 1 real gap and 24 labeled unverified rather than guessed, backed by 16 tests each built from a real transaction shape. Closing an opportunity is confirmed by re-reading the record, never by the write's status code. Invoices were added to the shipping scanner only after measuring them at 95.1% tracked once never-ship lines were removed.
